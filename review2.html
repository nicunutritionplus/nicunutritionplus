Human milk and vitamin D as modifiers of neonatal brain injury: a scoping review

Summary of the evidence

The evidence that more human milk attenuates the progression of brain injury in preterm infants is consistent in direction but weak in design, and the evidence for vitamin D is almost entirely indirect. Observational cohorts repeatedly associate higher doses of mother's own milk (MOM) with lower rates of severe intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) or periventricular leukomalacia (PVL), more mature white matter at term-equivalent age, and better cognition; however, the two adequately powered randomized trials of donor milk versus preterm formula found no neurodevelopmental benefit, and no trial has used IVH progression, post-hemorrhagic ventricular dilatation (PHVD) or white matter injury (WMI) as a primary outcome. For vitamin D, preclinical models of hypoxia-ischemia and intracerebral hemorrhage show reproducible neuroprotection, and low 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] tracks with IVH and with more severe hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE), but randomized trials in preterm infants were designed around bone and respiratory endpoints and none has tested brain injury.

The most defensible reading is that neither exposure plausibly prevents the initial germinal matrix hemorrhage, most of which occurs before enteral intake is meaningful, but that both could act on the slower secondary processes — inflammation-driven white matter injury, PHVD and impaired brain maturation — where the timing of exposure and injury overlap (Figure 3). This hypothesis is testable and has not been tested.

Background and objectives

Germinal matrix–intraventricular hemorrhage remains the most frequent acquired brain lesion of very preterm infants, and its long-term burden comes as much from what follows the bleed — parenchymal venous infarction, PHVD, and diffuse WMI with oligodendrocyte dysmaturation — as from the bleed itself (Yates et al. 2021). In term infants with HIE, therapeutic hypothermia addresses only part of the secondary and tertiary injury cascade. Both conditions share mechanisms that nutrition could plausibly modulate: systemic inflammation and sepsis, oxidative stress, blood–brain barrier disruption, microglial activation, and deficits in myelination substrates.

This review maps the evidence for and against two nutritional exposures: (1) greater provision of human milk, including MOM, donor human milk (DHM), exclusive human milk diets (EHMD), oropharyngeal colostrum, intranasal milk, and isolated milk bioactives; and (2) vitamin D status or supplementation, maternal or infant. Following the PCC framework, the population was preterm infants and term infants with brain injury (IVH, PVL/WMI, PHVD, HIE); the concept was the effect of the exposure on brain-injury incidence, severity, progression, MRI-defined brain structure, neurodevelopment, or mechanism; and the context was any setting, including preclinical models where they inform mechanism.

Methods

Search and selection

PubMed was searched with 12 structured queries combining MeSH and free-text terms for human milk (or its components) or vitamin D with IVH/PVL/WMI/PHVD, HIE, brain MRI, neurodevelopment, and neuroprotection (query strings in the Appendix); searches were run in October 2026. The searches returned 1,006 hits (887 unique PMIDs; 881 with retrievable metadata). Targeted searches added seven landmark records not captured by the keyword strategy (MILK, DoMINO, the two Vohr reports, Belfort, Isaacs, and maternal vitamin D supplementation during lactation), and four records excluded at screening were re-included on verification (three preterm vitamin D dosing trials and one vitamin D receptor genotype study).

Screening and charting

Title/abstract screening and data charting used a single, pre-specified eligibility rubric applied by a large-language-model screener, followed by investigator verification of every study cited in this narrative; records the screener could not process (n = 85) were screened manually on title. For each included record we charted exposure, population, design, outcome domain, direction of effect (benefit, null, mixed, harm), sample size, putative mechanism, and a one-sentence key finding. In keeping with scoping-review methodology, no formal risk-of-bias assessment or meta-analysis was performed. Because charting was automated, the direction-of-effect codes in the evidence table should be treated as a map for orientation rather than as verified data extraction.

Results

Evidence map

Three hundred records were charted: 100 on human milk feeding, 72 on human-milk bioactives, 121 on vitamin D, and 7 on other related exposures (Figure 1). The two literatures have nearly opposite shapes (Figure 2). Human milk evidence is predominantly clinical — 30 RCTs or systematic reviews and 41 observational or early-phase studies, but only two preclinical studies of whole milk. Vitamin D evidence is predominantly preclinical (52 animal or in vitro studies, most in adult stroke, traumatic brain injury or hemorrhage models), with only four human studies addressing preterm IVH and 11 addressing HIE. Across human studies of human milk and preterm IVH/WMI/PHVD, roughly three-quarters of directional studies reported benefit, whereas studies of isolated milk bioactives (lactoferrin, DHA/ARA) mostly reported null effects on brain outcomes.

Human milk and preterm IVH incidence and severity

Observational studies consistently associate more human milk with less severe IVH, but the exposure is usually measured after most hemorrhages have occurred. In a cohort of 306 extremely low birth weight infants, an exclusive human milk diet was associated with fewer cases of severe IVH or PVL (7% vs 18%) (Carome et al. 2021); high-proportion breast milk feeding in the first week was associated with less grade I–II IVH among 604 very preterm infants (Zheng et al. 2022); and receipt of bank milk by day 7 was associated with lower IVH odds after milk-bank implementation (Torres-Muñoz et al. 2021). Exclusive maternal milk feeding was associated with fewer PVL cases in a smaller cohort (Cortez et al. 2018), and a multifaceted quality-improvement program that raised human milk use coincided with fewer cases of severe IVH across a large network (Ellsbury et al. 2016).

These associations are vulnerable to reverse causation and confounding by illness severity. An infant who bleeds on day 2 is more likely to be unstable, to have feeds withheld, and to be classified as low-milk exposure at day 7, so exposure windows that end after the period of hemorrhage cannot separate cause from consequence. Other cohorts found no association between feeding type and IVH or PVL (Kiechl-Kohlendorfer et al. 2018; Lin et al. 2025). One retrospective comparison even found more grade 3–4 IVH in DHM-fed than in MOM-fed infants (19.6% vs 5.5%), which most plausibly reflects who receives donor milk rather than a harm of donor milk (Velumula et al. 2023).

Randomized evidence on very early colostrum exposure, the only form of human milk that coincides with the window of hemorrhage, is inconsistent. One trial of oropharyngeal colostrum in 252 infants ≤32 weeks reported less severe IVH (1.6% vs 7.2%; RR 0.22, 95% CI 0.05–0.99) (OuYang et al. 2021), whereas other trials found no difference in IVH (Aggarwal et al. 2021; Sudeep et al. 2022; Tehranchi et al. 2024). Pooled analyses show a borderline effect on grade ≥3 IVH (RR 0.44, 95% CI 0.19–1.01) and no effect on PVL (Huo et al. 2022), and the largest meta-analysis confirmed reductions in sepsis, NEC and mortality but not in IVH (Anne et al. 2024). A network meta-analysis of 59 fortification RCTs ranked MOM-based regimens highest for PVL, although rankings rest on sparse, indirect comparisons (Campbell-Yeo et al. 2025).

Human milk and injury progression

The most direct test of progression comes from intranasal administration of fresh breast milk, a strategy premised on delivering milk-derived neurotrophins, growth factors and stem cells across the nasal–olfactory route. In a retrospective series of 31 very low birth weight infants with grade 3–4 IVH, intranasal milk was associated with fewer severe porencephalic defects (21% vs 58%) (Keller et al. 2019). A phase 1 trial in 37 preterm infants with IVH found it feasible and safe (Hoban et al. 2024), with non-significant favorable neurodevelopmental trends versus historical controls (Gallipoli et al. 2025), and a 22-infant study reported less progression to PHVD and more IVH regression (Sonmez Demir et al. 2025). Preclinical work supports the route: intranasal colostrum-derived extracellular vesicles reached the neonatal rat brain and partially normalized LPS-induced glial activation (Ture et al. 2026), and breast-milk stem cells improved myelination in rat WMI (Zhang et al. 2025). All clinical data are small, unblinded or historically controlled, so the signal is hypothesis-generating only.

Human milk and brain structure

MRI studies support a dose-dependent association between human milk and white matter maturation, which is the substrate of injury progression. Each additional day of predominant breast milk in the first 28 days was associated with 0.15 cc more deep nuclear grey matter at term-equivalent age and 0.5 IQ points at age 7 (Belfort et al. 2016). Breast milk exposure was linked to advanced corpus callosum maturation on diffusion tensor imaging (Pogribna et al. 2013) and to greater structural connectivity and fractional anisotropy, with a dose effect at ≥90% exposure (Blesa et al. 2019). In adolescents born preterm, the proportion of breast milk received correlated with verbal IQ and, in boys, with white matter volume (Isaacs et al. 2010). A 2026 cohort found that DHM-fed infants resembled MOM-fed infants on brain volume and white matter microstructure, whereas formula-fed infants did not (Ottolini et al. 2026). Systematic reviews describe the association as consistent but small, and disagree on whether it is larger in term or in preterm infants (Ottolini et al. 2020; Gilbreath et al. 2024). The MRI literature rarely stratifies by baseline IVH grade, so whether milk modifies the trajectory of an injured brain, as opposed to a brain at risk, remains unknown.

Human milk and neurodevelopment

The divergence between observational and randomized evidence is the central argument against a causal neuroprotective effect. Cohort data show dose–response gradients: each 10 mL/kg/day of breast milk in the NICU was associated with +0.53 to +0.59 Bayley mental development points at 18–30 months (Vohr et al. 2006; Vohr et al. 2007), and higher MOM doses in critical exposure windows were associated with higher cognitive scores at 20 months (Meier 2019). A pooled analysis likewise found higher cognitive scores and less neurodevelopmental impairment with breastfeeding in preterm infants (Zhang et al. 2024).

Randomized trials of donor milk, the only feasible randomized contrast to MOM, have not reproduced these gradients. In DoMINO, supplemental DHM versus preterm formula did not improve Bayley-III scores at 18 months in 363 VLBW infants (O'Connor et al. 2016), and in the MILK trial (483 extremely preterm infants with minimal maternal milk) cognitive scores at 22–26 months did not differ, despite less NEC with donor milk (4.2% vs 9.0%) (Colaizy et al. 2024). Cochrane and meta-analytic syntheses agree that DHM and MOM reduce NEC, but find little or no effect on neurodevelopment (Quigley et al. 2024; Strobel et al. 2022). Two readings remain open: the observational benefit could be confounding by maternal and socioeconomic factors, or the active ingredients could be specific to fresh MOM and lost in pasteurized, pooled DHM. A secondary analysis of a historical trial is consistent with the second reading through an infection pathway — higher breast milk intake reduced infection and NEC, which in turn mediated better performance IQ into adulthood (Lapidaire et al. 2022).

Human milk in term HIE

The HIE literature addresses safety of feeding during hypothermia rather than neuroprotection. A UK population cohort found enteral feeding during therapeutic hypothermia was associated with less NEC and infection, higher survival, and more breastfeeding at discharge (Gale et al. 2021), and a multicentre cohort confirmed low NEC rates (De Rose et al. 2026). Intranasal breast milk is feasible in moderate-to-severe HIE (Tarjanyi et al. 2026). No study in HIE has related milk dose to MRI injury or neurodevelopment.

Human-milk bioactives

Isolated bioactives generate strong preclinical signals but neutral clinical results on brain outcomes. Maternal lactoferrin supplementation reduced cortical injury, normalized white matter microstructure and dampened TNF-α/IL-6 in rat pups after hypoxia-ischemia (van de Looij et al. 2014) or LPS exposure (Ginet et al. 2016), acted synergistically with hypothermia after neonatal hypoxia-ischemia (Sanches et al. 2023), and preserved mitochondrial function via Nrf2 (Carvalho et al. 2025). A U-shaped dose response was evident: 1 g/kg was protective but 10 g/kg caused hypomyelination (Sanches et al. 2021). Clinically, however, the LIFT trial (n = 1,542) showed no reduction in death or major morbidity including brain injury (Tarnow-Mordi et al. 202030093-6)), and meta-analysis found no effect on IVH (Gao et al. 2020) or neurodevelopmental impairment (Pammi et al. 2021). One cohort reported larger total brain volumes with high milk lactoferrin exposure (Atayde et al. 2024).

Long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids show a similar pattern. Enteral DHA ± ARA did not affect IVH or PVL in a meta-analysis of 11 RCTs but increased bronchopulmonary dysplasia (Dang et al. 2025). ARA:DHA improved white matter fractional anisotropy in infants <29 weeks (Moltu et al. 2024) without changing Bayley-III scores at 2 years (Gunnarsdottir et al. 2025). Human milk oligosaccharides attenuated NEC-associated brain injury via BDNF in neonatal mice (Sodhi et al. 2023) and rescued cortical myelination in a double-hit preterm brain injury model (Brandt et al. 2025), while clinical associations between individual HMOs and outcome are inconsistent in direction (Wejryd et al. 2025). A milk oxysterol (20α-hydroxycholesterol) induced oligodendrogenesis and improved myelination in a neonatal WMI model (Chao et al. 2023). The gap between preclinical and clinical effects suggests either that whole milk acts through combinations of components, or that the relevant mechanism in humans is indirect (prevention of sepsis and NEC, which are themselves major drivers of WMI).

Vitamin D and preterm IVH

Human evidence linking vitamin D to IVH is limited to cross-sectional associations. Cord blood 25(OH)D was lower in preterm infants with IVH than without (15.9 vs 23.7 ng/mL) (Boskabadi et al. 2018), whereas vitamin D receptor polymorphisms were associated with BPD and NEC rather than IVH (Kosik et al. 2020). Low cord 25(OH)D is also a marker of maternal deprivation, preeclampsia and preterm delivery itself, so these associations cannot be attributed to vitamin D.

Randomized trials in preterm infants answer a different question. Trials comparing 0, 200, 400 and 800 IU/day established that 800 IU/day corrects biochemical deficiency (Natarajan et al. 2014; Fort et al. 2016) and that standard dosing can occasionally produce potentially toxic concentrations (Kołodziejczyk-Nowotarska et al. 2021). The only follow-up powered toward neurodevelopment showed a non-significant linear trend toward less impairment with increasing dose (54%, 43%, 30%; p = 0.08) (Salas et al. 2018). An early 800 IU/day regimen in human-milk-fed extremely preterm infants did not reduce BPD severity (Salas et al. 2025), and the ViDES trial will report 2-year neurodevelopment (Romero-Lopez et al. 2024). Systematic reviews, including the 2026 Cochrane review of 34 trials (5,012 infants), report little or no effect on neurodevelopment and do not report IVH or WMI as outcomes (Kumar et al. 2022; Pharande et al. 2026; Bronnert et al. 2025; Shin et al. 2026). In the broader vitamin meta-analysis, IVH was reduced by vitamin E, not vitamin D (Bronnert et al. 2025).

Vitamin D and term HIE

Vitamin D evidence in HIE is more mechanistically coherent. Most HIE neonates have insufficient 25(OH)D, which falls further over the first 72 hours regardless of hypothermia (Lowe et al. 2017); lower neonatal 25(OH)D correlated with greater MRI injury severity (McGinn et al. 2020), worse developmental quotients (Liu et al. 2022), and mortality in severe perinatal depression (Batra et al. 2023). Whether low vitamin D is a cause or a consequence of injury is unresolved, because hypoxia-ischemia itself induces the catabolic enzyme CYP24A1 in brain and lowers serum vitamin D in rats (Lowe et al. 2017). Interventional data are early-phase: an open-label phase I study of N-acetylcysteine plus vitamin D during hypothermia reduced CNS oxidative stress, and the 24 treated infants with follow-up showed no cerebral palsy at 2–4 years (Jenkins et al. 2021), while a 90-infant trial reported lower serum S100B after adjunctive vitamin D in Sarnat II HIE (Hagag et al. 2019).

Maternal and antenatal vitamin D

Maternal 25(OH)D sets fetal and early neonatal stores, before infant supplementation can act. Maternal insufficiency was associated with lower offspring mental and language scores in a meta-analysis (Tous et al. 2020), but cord-blood cohorts show null (McCarthy et al. 2018) or nonlinear associations, and high-dose prenatal supplementation did not improve cognition at age 2 (Sass et al. 2020). Because human milk is low in vitamin D, these two exposures interact: maternal supplementation with 6,400 IU/day achieved infant 25(OH)D equivalent to direct infant supplementation (Hollis et al. 2015), offering a route that combines milk provision and vitamin D repletion.

Preclinical vitamin D neuroprotection

Animal models consistently show neuroprotection by vitamin D in injuries that resemble neonatal brain injury, but most come from adult rodents. In neonatal hypoxia-ischemia, calcitriol post-treatment reduced brain damage in 7-day-old rats (Kajta et al. 2009), vitamin D added to hypothermia and NAC improved sensorimotor and cognitive function (Lowe et al. 2017), and vitamin D activated Nrf2/HO-1 and suppressed ferroptosis (Cai et al. 2022). In hemorrhagic injury — the closest analogue to IVH progression — vitamin D deficiency increased hematoma expansion and blood–brain barrier leakage after intracerebral hemorrhage, which acute calcitriol reversed (Chan et al. 2024); vitamin D also accelerated hematoma clearance by promoting reparative CD36+ macrophages (Liu et al. 2022), protected brain endothelial barrier function via VDR-dependent inhibition of NF-κB and MMP-9 (Won et al. 2015), and reduced brain edema after subarachnoid hemorrhage (Enkhjargal et al. 2017). Microglial VDR deletion worsened ischemic injury through TNF-α/IFN-γ-driven inflammation (Cui et al. 2023). A biphasic dose response is a recurring caveat: neuroprotection at low concentrations turns to toxicity at higher ones in neurons in vitro (Brewer et al. 2001; Loginova et al. 2021).

Discussion

Evidence in favor

The case for benefit rests on consistency across levels of evidence rather than on any single decisive study. Dose–response gradients appear in independent cohorts, MRI studies localize associations to white matter and deep grey matter (the regions most damaged after preterm IVH and HIE), and randomized trials establish that human milk reduces NEC and late-onset sepsis, two of the strongest modifiable drivers of preterm WMI. For vitamin D, the convergence is mechanistic: preclinical studies in hemorrhagic and hypoxic-ischemic models consistently implicate VDR-dependent anti-inflammatory, antioxidant and barrier-protective pathways, and clinical HIE cohorts link lower 25(OH)D to MRI-defined injury severity.

Evidence against

Five considerations weigh against inferring that either exposure attenuates brain injury. First, timing: roughly 90% of germinal matrix hemorrhages occur within 72 hours, before enteral intake is substantial, so effects on incidence are implausible except possibly for oropharyngeal colostrum, where trials disagree. Second, confounding and reverse causation: sicker infants are fed later and receive less milk, and maternal milk provision tracks socioeconomic advantage. Third, the randomized trials that could test milk-specific neuroprotection (DoMINO, MILK) were null for neurodevelopment, and the lactoferrin and DHA programs that isolated plausible active components did not reduce brain injury. Fourth, no RCT in either domain has used IVH progression, PHVD, or MRI-defined WMI as a primary endpoint, and most studies do not stratify by baseline injury. Fifth, the dose response for vitamin D appears biphasic in vitro and supplementation can overshoot in preterm infants, so higher doses are not necessarily safer.

Interpretation

Taken together, the evidence supports a narrow, testable hypothesis rather than a recommendation: in preterm infants who have already sustained IVH, maximizing fresh MOM and correcting vitamin D deficiency may limit secondary inflammatory and oxidative injury, PHVD, and white matter dysmaturation. Pasteurized DHM may not reproduce the effects of fresh MOM. The infection pathway is the best-supported causal link for human milk. For vitamin D, hematoma clearance and barrier protection are the most specific candidate mechanisms for IVH, but they are currently supported only by adult rodent hemorrhage models.

Research gaps

  1. No randomized trial of human milk dose, intranasal milk, or vitamin D has enrolled infants after diagnosis of IVH with PHVD, ventricular index trajectory, or term MRI WMI as the primary outcome; the intranasal data (n ≤ 37 per study) are the closest and are uncontrolled.
  2. No preterm vitamin D trial has reported cranial ultrasound or MRI outcomes, although the ViDES design would allow secondary reporting of IVH and WMI.
  3. Preclinical vitamin D studies of germinal matrix hemorrhage or neonatal intraventricular blood (e.g., collagenase or glycerol models in neonatal rabbits or rats) were not identified; the hemorrhage evidence comes from adult intracerebral hemorrhage models.
  4. Observational human milk–IVH studies define exposure after the hemorrhage window; analyses that use exposure windows beginning after the IVH diagnosis, or target-trial emulation, are needed to separate effects on progression from reverse causation.
  5. The interaction between milk source and vitamin D is unstudied: exclusively MOM-fed infants receive little vitamin D from milk, and maternal high-dose supplementation has not been tested in mothers of preterm infants.
  6. In term HIE, no study relates milk dose during or after hypothermia to MRI injury or neurodevelopment, and vitamin D adjunct data are phase I or small single-centre trials.

Conclusion

Human milk, particularly fresh MOM, has the stronger clinical evidence base, with consistent observational associations with less severe IVH/PVL, better white matter maturation and better cognition. However, the randomized donor milk trials were null for neurodevelopment, and no trial has targeted injury progression. Vitamin D has the stronger mechanistic rationale for post-hemorrhagic and hypoxic-ischemic injury but almost no clinical brain-injury data in preterm infants. Neither exposure should be expected to prevent initial IVH. Both are low-cost, already part of standard care, and compatible with a factorial trial in infants with established IVH that uses PHVD and term MRI as outcomes.
